# ADR 0015: Engines on other computers, reached through a command; Xsuite on threads and GPUs

Status: accepted (2026-10-18). Scope: engine hosts (`eicvibe.engines.remote`, `eicvibe-engine serve`, `EngineWorker(host=)`, amending ADR 0006's decision 8), Xsuite's compute context (`EICVIBE_XSUITE_CONTEXT`), and the virtual ring's and route's host (`RingRun`, `RouteRun`, `eicvibe-virtual --host`, amending ADR 0007).

## Context

The virtual accelerator tracks each machine in an engine worker, a process of the server's computer (ADR 0006), and Xsuite tracks there on one thread. The RCS's ramp (ADR 0013) takes 7.5 ms a turn with 20 particles on the M3 Max the service runs on, so 3.5 minutes a ramp. Profiled, 92 % of it is Xsuite's tracking kernel; the work around it each turn (the moving reference, the RF program, the BPM monitors) is 0.5 ms. More speed must come from more cores or a GPU, and it can only come with more particles: they share out the particles, not the turns.

The computers at hand: FRIB's accphy1 (`ssh aphpc`, through FRIB's SSH gateway: two 16-core EPYC 7313, 1 TB, four A100 40 GB, shared with others), and BNL SDCC's Institutional Cluster (A100 nodes behind its SSH gateways and Slurm; for its stakeholders, others through a liaison; a 30-minute debug partition). Xsuite tracks on OpenMP threads (`ContextCpu(omp_num_threads)`) and on NVIDIA GPUs (`ContextCupy`) with the same code; its beam monitor adds up its moments with atomic operations.

## Decisions

1. **An engine host is a command.** It starts `eicvibe-engine serve` where the engine is to run (`ssh aphpc '... eicvibe-engine serve'`; through a gateway and a scheduler, `ssh -J gateway submit srun ... eicvibe-engine serve`), and its standard input and output carry the engine worker's calls and their results. Nothing opens a port: the connection is the command's, with the keys, tickets and gateways it already uses, and the server ends when its input does. `EngineWorker(engine, host)` sends its calls there instead of to a process of its own (`EngineHost`, `RemoteEngine`); nothing above the worker changes.
2. **Messages carry data, never code.** A message is a JSON header and the raw bytes of the arrays it names. The header holds plain data, tuples, sets, dicts, numpy arrays and the engine contract's own types (`eicvibe.engines.contracts`: its models, dataclasses and enums), named and rebuilt from that list; a message naming anything else is refused where it is read. Errors cross as the contract's (`UnsupportedRequest`, `ParticleLost`, `EngineUnavailable`), as `ValueError` and `KeyError`, or as `RuntimeError` with their message. Both ends first say which contract they speak (a digest of the modules defining it): a host with another copy of EICViBE is refused, and named in the error. The server's standard output carries only messages (what Xsuite or Julia print goes to standard error, which the client logs), and text a shell prints before the server starts, such as a login banner, is skipped and logged.
3. **A lost host is a lost worker.** The calls waiting fail with `BrokenProcessPool`, as when a local worker dies, and so does a session's next call once the process it lives in is gone (a local worker's too: a session whose worker had died between calls failed with `KeyError`); the next call starts the command again. The virtual ring opens a new session and goes on from the same beam, which it holds between calls (an `engine restarted` event). A host refused (another contract, or a hello that fails, such as a GPU context without CuPy) is not retried: `RemoteEngineError`.
4. **Xsuite tracks bunches where `EICVIBE_XSUITE_CONTEXT` says**: `cpu`, the default, on one thread; `cpu:N` or `cpu:auto` on OpenMP threads; `cuda` or `cuda:N` on a GPU, with CuPy; `cuda:N,M,...` on several (2026-10-20): a bunch of at least `PER_GPU` (25 000) particles a GPU is shared out among them, each share on a line of its own on its GPU, tracked at once in a thread each, the monitors' counts and centroids combined by count and the final coordinates put back in order; a smaller bunch stays on the first. GPU kernels are compiled fully optimized (`xobjects.settings.cuda_fast_compile` off, unless `XSUITE_CUDA_FAST_COMPILE` is set): 1.3 to 1.8 times as fast as Xsuite's default fast compile on an A100, for a compilation of about nine minutes for each kernel (a line's element types and monitors), which CuPy keeps (`CUPY_CACHE_DIR`; a second start loads it in a second). `eicvibe-engine serve --xsuite-context` sets it on a host. Only bunches (`track_beam`: the ring's turns, a route's shots) go there; optics, orbits and `track` follow a particle or a few and stay on one thread. Each particle tracks alone, so its coordinates are the same, bit for bit, on GPUs of a kind and shared out among them, and on CPU threads; a GPU's and a CPU's differ by rounding (1e-13 after 40 turns of the demo ring: a GPU fuses multiplications and additions). The BPMs' centroids are added up in another order, the same to 1e-12. Arrays set on a GPU's elements (multipole coefficients) are moved to its memory first. Quantum excitation is Xsuite's `quantum-kick` model (2026-10-19): the photons a slice emits counted, their total energy drawn from Xsuite's tables, where `quantum` draws each photon's energy by rejection; the same energy loss (one turn of the RCS at 0.75 GeV, 20 000 particles: mean delta -7.90e-7 and -7.91e-7, spread 1.679e-7 and 1.690e-7), ten times faster on an A100 and as fast on a CPU (no photon records, which nothing here reads).
5. **A run says where its engine runs.** `RingRun(host=)`, `RouteRun(host=)`, `eicvibe-virtual ring|route --host COMMAND`; the run's info records the host's name.

## Where to track (measured on accphy1, Validation)

- Few particles (the virtual accelerator's 20): one thread, and the service's own computer is fastest (an M3 Max core is 1.7 times an EPYC's).
- Hundreds to tens of thousands of particles: OpenMP threads on the host (`cpu:32`).
- A hundred thousand and more: the GPU, or several (`cuda:1,3`: 1.8 to 1.9 times one at fixed energy), 170 to 220 times an EPYC core without radiation or with its mean, and about 140 times with quantum excitation since it is `quantum-kick` (402 ms a turn for 100 000 particles of the RCS: seven times 32 threads).

## Setting up a host

- A copy of EICViBE the same as the service's (the contract's digest checks it), with Python 3.14: `uv sync --frozen --no-dev --extra xsuite`; for a GPU, `uv pip install "cupy-cuda12x[ctk]"`, which brings CUDA's libraries from PyPI (no CUDA toolkit; a driver for CUDA 12).
- On accphy1 all of it is in one folder, `/home/haoy/Research/VA_test`, with `env.sh` pointing every cache there (uv, Python, CuPy's kernels, CUDA's, TMPDIR) and `CUDA_DEVICE_ORDER=PCI_BUS_ID`, so that `cuda:N` is nvidia-smi's GPU N. Its command:
  `ssh -o ServerAliveInterval=30 aphpc 'cd /home/haoy/Research/VA_test && . ./env.sh && exec EICViBE/.venv/bin/eicvibe-engine serve --xsuite-context cuda:1'`
- `ServerAliveInterval` is needed: FRIB's gateway drops a connection that carries nothing for a while (a benchmark printing nothing for 50 minutes lost its connection, and its output).
- On SDCC, the same through its gateway and `srun` on an A100 partition the account may use (not tried).

## Not done yet

- The service: engine hosts in its configuration, a machine's host chosen on the admin page and shown in its table.
- TrackPad on a host (Julia and TrackPad installed there, its depot in the host's folder): untested.
- Batch runs on a scheduler: `eicvibe-virtual` there, writing run folders the viewer reads from shared storage.
- OpenMP's kernels compile in each server process, about three minutes on accphy1 for each set, and a ring needs two (its matching's, without quantum excitation, and its tracking's); they are not kept between processes. A GPU's optimized kernel takes nine minutes once, then CuPy's cache.
- Small bunches on threads: below about 100 particles one thread is faster (Validation), so a session could keep its bunches there whatever the context.
- A host command names its GPUs; nothing checks whether others are using them.
- More than two GPUs: measured on two (accphy1's others held another user's sessions). A ramp's per-turn reference update on several GPUs (Xsuite's `update_p0c_and_energy_deviations`, masked) could be the engine's own, unmasked, to overlap fully.

## Upstream finding (Xsuite: xtrack 0.115.5, xobjects 0.7.1, CuPy 14.2, A100)

Quantum excitation costs nothing more than the mean radiation on a CPU (513 against 511 ms a turn for 1000 particles of the RCS, one core), and 13 times as much on an A100 (3892 against 303 ms a turn for 100 000 particles; 52 us a particle and turn, about 11 EPYC cores' worth, where without radiation it is 2.1 us, 216 cores' worth). The photons' sampling, element by element, seems to be what the GPU does badly (branches that differ from particle to particle, random-number state in global memory). Reproduced with `bench/gpu_physics.py` on accphy1: the RCS of the EIN machine folder, fixed energy, no monitors. Xsuite's own `quantum-kick` avoids it (402 against 3966 ms a turn), and EICViBE uses it since 2026-10-19; `quantum` remains slow there, for whoever needs its photons.

## Validation (2026-10-18)

- `tests/engines/test_remote.py`: the contract's types cross unchanged (NaN, tuple keys, arrays of any dtype); other types are refused both ways; a server run as a host would run it tracks a bunch the same as a local worker; a lost host fails what waits and the next call starts it again; a banner is skipped; a host with another contract is refused.
- On accphy1, the engine tests (conformance suite and remote) pass with `EICVIBE_XSUITE_CONTEXT` unset, `cpu:8` and `cuda:1` (A100).
- From the M3 Max through FRIB's gateway, a call costs 45 ms of round trip; the rest is the engine's own work (one EPYC core is 1.7 times slower than an M3 Max's).
- The RCS run end to end (`eicvibe-virtual ring --host ... --xsuite-context cpu:32`, 1000 particles, ramped at turn 80): matched, injected, ramped, its BPM history written here; 15 turns a second (67 ms a turn: 46 ms of tracking and the round trip over calls of 16 turns), after five and a half minutes of compiling. It showed the fault of ADR 0013's ramp corrected there (2026-10-18): a horizontal line at 1/16, the calls' period.
- A whole RCS ramp of 1000 particles through the host (cpu:32), driven from the M3 Max: the Mac slept again and again (it was left alone), each sleep froze the run and FRIB's gateway dropped the connection, 11 times; each time the ring started the engine again on accphy1 (compiling anew) and went on from the same beam, and the ramp ended with its bunch extracted (ADR 0013). It took 17 hours where it would have taken 35 minutes: a long run is for a computer that stays awake, or for the host itself (`eicvibe-virtual` there).
- Xsuite's GPU kernel is one for the whole line and all the turns of a call: each thread carries a particle through every element, so launches cost nothing (20 one-turn calls take what one 20-turn call does). The kernel is the limit: 254 registers a thread, the most there are, and 2 KB spilled, so 256 threads a multiprocessor (12.5 % occupancy). Capping its registers is slower (128: 1.4 times, 96: 1.55 times: more spills). Compiled fully optimized it is 1.3 to 1.8 times as fast as Xsuite's default fast compile (the RCS, quantum-kick, no monitors: 220 to 257 against 393 ms a turn for 100 000 particles, 2529 against 3669 for 1 000 000; without radiation 162 against 204).
- The 192 BPMs' monitors cost about a third on a GPU (331 against about 240 ms a turn for 100 000 particles): every thread adds into the same sums with atomic operations. Xsuite's own clear and read of them take a launch or a copy for every statistic of every monitor (0.45 s and 0.21 s a call for 192 monitors); the engine clears and reads only the three sums it uses, those at once (0.04 s).
- Two A100s (`cuda:1,3`) against one, the RCS with 192 BPMs and quantum excitation, ms a turn: 100 000 particles 183 against 331 (fixed energy) and 183 against 314 (ramping); 1 000 000 particles 1632 against 3058 and 2078 against 3016. The final coordinates, losses and counts are those of one GPU, bit for bit, and the centroids to 1.5e-18 m. The threads overlap only when the engine waits for a GPU with `synchronize` before reading it: reading waits too, but holding Python's lock, which serialized them (443 against 391 ms before). A ramp's per-turn reference update (Xsuite's, with masks) still holds it partly.
- The A100 (`cuda:1`), the RCS's ramp with quantum excitation, ms a turn: 297, 505, 798, 5474 and 51 881 with 20, 1000, 10 000, 100 000 and 1 000 000 particles (52 us a particle and turn: slower than 32 threads' 27), most of it the tracking kernel (profiled: the time waits in the copies back from the GPU). At fixed energy with 100 000 particles: 211 ms a turn without radiation, 303 with its mean, 3892 with quantum excitation; the fast fidelity 189 and 2924.
- The RCS's ramp on accphy1 (mid ramp, radiation and quantum excitation, 192 BPMs, ms a turn): one EPYC core 13.3 with 20 particles and 580 with 1000 (the M3 Max: 7.1 with 20); 16 threads 59, 57, 422 and 4061 with 20, 1000, 10 000 and 100 000 particles; 32 threads 62, 46, 309 and 2680. Threads share out large bunches well (14 times on 16 threads at 100 000 particles; 21 times on 32, half of them hyper-threads), but Xsuite's OpenMP kernel costs about 42 ms a turn whatever the bunch (as much tracking 50 turns in one call: not EICViBE's per-turn work), so they lose below about 100 particles. A ramp of 1000 particles takes 18 minutes on 32 threads, where one core would take 4 hours.
